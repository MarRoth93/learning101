    From “what are all these pins?” to wiring — and explaining — a button and an LED

    By the end of this module you will build the circuit below on a real ESP32 board, and you'll be able to say why every wire is where it is — and what would go wrong if it weren't.

    What you'll be able to do

    1. Name the board's main parts (USB port, USB-to-serial chip, regulator, module, EN/BOOT buttons, headers) and explain what each does. Ch 2
    2. Sort pins into categories — power, ground, general GPIO, input-only, boot-sensitive, serial, flash-reserved — and choose a suitable pin for a job. Ch 3
    3. Wire an LED output and justify it: polarity, the resistor and its value, and the return path to ground. Ch 1, 5
    4. Wire a button input and justify it: pull-up or pull-down, “pressed reads 0”, and why an unconnected input misbehaves. Ch 6
    5. Predict and diagnose: say what a given wiring or code mistake will look like, and trace an unexpected symptom back to its cause. Ch 7, 8, 9

    Which board this lesson assumes

    Assumed board: Espressif ESP32-DevKitC V4 with an ESP32-WROOM-32E module (38 pins, two rows of 19). Pin numbers, layouts and boot rules below are checked against Espressif's DevKitC user guide and the ESP32 datasheet.

    Many shops sell look-alikes (“ESP32 DevKit V1”, 30-pin boards, NodeMCU-32S…). The principles in this lesson apply to all of them; the pin positions may not. Before wiring, check:

    1. The chip family printed on the metal shield. This lesson covers the original ESP32 (WROOM-32, -32D, -32E, -32UE). ESP32-S2, -S3, -C3, -C6 boards have different pin numbers and different boot pins — don't reuse the numbers here.
    2. The labels printed next to your pins. Trust the printed label (e.g. “IO23”, “D23” or “G23”) over any picture, including this one.
    3. Which module you have: on WROVER modules GPIO16/17 are reserved; on WROOM they're free.
    4. Whether there's a user LED. The DevKitC has only a power LED; many clones add a blue LED on GPIO2.

    What you need

    • ESP32 DevKitC (or compatible) and a USB cable that carries data (some cables only charge)
    • Solderless breadboard and a few male–male jumper wires
    • 1 LED (red is easiest), 1 resistor of 220 Ω or 330 Ω, 1 push button (the common 6 mm, 4-leg type)
    • Optional: a 10 kΩ resistor and a multimeter

    Chapter 1 · Prerequisites

    Just enough electricity

    Concrete question: an LED has two legs. Why can't you just touch one leg to a battery and have it light?

    Three quantities, one picture

    Voltage (V, volts) is the electrical “push” between two points. A battery labelled 3 V keeps its + side 3 V above its − side. Voltage is always between two places — a single point doesn't “have” a voltage until you say “compared to what”.

    Current (I, amperes, A) is how much charge flows past a point per second. Small circuits use milliamperes: 1 mA = 0.001 A. An indicator LED needs a few mA.

    Resistance (R, ohms, Ω) is how strongly something opposes current. A resistor is a part made to have a specific resistance, e.g. 220 Ω.

    Circuit = a closed loop. Current leaves the source's + side, passes through the parts, and returns to the − side. Break the loop anywhere and current stops everywhere.

    Ground (GND) is the point we agree to call 0 V — the shared reference and return path. On the ESP32 board, every pin labelled GND is connected together and to the − side of the supply. When we later say “GPIO23 is at 3.3 V”, we mean “3.3 V above GND”.

    An analogy — and where it breaks

    Water systemElectric circuit
    Pump creating a pressure differenceVoltage source (battery, USB, a GPIO pin set HIGH)
    Flow rate (litres per second)Current (amperes)
    Narrow section of pipeResistor
    One-way valveDiode — an LED is a diode that emits light
    Pipe back to the pump's inletReturn path to GND
    Closed valveOpen switch
    Where the analogy stops being accurate: a broken water pipe spills; a broken wire doesn't leak electricity — the current simply stops. Pipes must fill up before water flows; wires are always “full”, so current starts everywhere in the loop at once. And “ground” on your board is not the earth outside — it is just the board's own 0 V reference. Use the analogy for intuition, but reason with the loop rule.

    Predict

    In a single loop, a switch opens between the resistor and the LED. Where does the current stop?

    The battery side is part of the same loop. Current can't pile up at the break — the charges have nowhere to go — so flow stops before the switch too. Misconception: thinking of current as water pouring into a dead-end pipe.
    A loop is all-or-nothing: current needs an unbroken path from + back to −. One gap anywhere stops it everywhere.
    There's no gradual slowing; the whole loop stops at once. Try it with the switch in the figure.
    Hint

    Can charge leave the battery's + side if it has no way to return to the − side?

    Apply

    You hold an LED's long leg on a battery's + terminal. The short leg touches nothing. What happens?

    There's no loop, so no current — not even a little. Faint glow would need some current to flow and return.
    Exactly. This is why every LED you wire to the ESP32 needs a second connection back to GND.
    Voltage is a difference between two points. With one leg unconnected, the LED doesn't have the battery's voltage across it.
    Electrons actually move the other way

    By an 18th-century convention, current is drawn flowing from + to − (“conventional current”). In metal wires, the charge carriers are electrons, which drift from − to +. For every calculation in this lesson the convention works perfectly, so we use it throughout.

    Chapter 2 · Physical parts

    Meet the board

    Concrete question: the ESP32 chip runs at 3.3 V, but your USB port supplies 5 V and talks “USB”. What's on the board that bridges the gap?

    Select a part (click, tap, or Tab + Enter). Then turn on the conceptual overlays to see how power and data travel between parts.

    Inside the module: an exploded view

    The big silver rectangle isn't the chip itself. It's a module: a small circuit board with the ESP32 chip and its helpers under a metal shield.

    Why does this step work?

    The USB connector delivers 5 V, yet the datasheet's absolute maximum for the ESP32 supply is 3.6 V. Why doesn't plugging in USB destroy the chip?

    The 5 V never reaches the chip directly. The voltage regulator (LDO) turns it into a steady 3.3 V, and only that 3.3 V supplies the module and the 3V3 pin. That's also why the 5V pin must never touch a GPIO: it bypasses the regulator.

    Self-check: did you mention (1) the regulator, (2) that it lowers 5 V to 3.3 V, (3) that the chip only sees the regulated side?

    Diagnose

    You plug in the board. The red power LED lights, but your computer shows no new serial port. Which part is the best first suspect?

    The power LED is lit, so 5 V is arriving; the regulator isn't the first suspect. Power and data are different paths — look at the data path overlay.
    Right. The power LED proves the power path works. A missing port points at the data path: cables without data wires, or a missing driver for the bridge chip (CP210x and CH340 are common).
    Possible, but unlikely first. The serial port is created by the USB-to-UART bridge, before the ESP32 is even involved.
    Hint

    Turn on both overlays. Which path is proven to work by the lit LED, and which path creates the “port”?

    Chapter 3 · Pin categories

    Pins and their categories

    Concrete question: the board has 38 pins. Which ones can you safely use for an LED and a button — and why are some off-limits?

    First, two words

    GPIO = general-purpose input/output: a pin your program controls. As an output, the pin is like a switch that connects itself either to 3.3 V (HIGH) or to GND (LOW). As an input, the pin measures the voltage someone else puts on it and reports HIGH or LOW.

    GPIO number vs position: in code, 23 means GPIO23 — the pin printed “IO23” — not the 23rd pin. On the DevKitC, IO23 sits second from the top on the right header.

    Rule that never changes: ESP32 pins work at 3.3 V. The datasheet's absolute maximum is 3.6 V. Never connect 5 V (including the 5V pin) to a GPIO.

    The categories in one table

    CategoryPins on DevKitCWhat it means for you
    General GPIO4, 13, 14, 16–19, 21–23, 25–27, 32, 33first choiceInput or output, internal pull-up/down available.
    Input-only34, 35, 36 (VP), 39 (VN)Can only read. No output driver, no internal pull-up/down.
    Boot-sensitive (strapping)0, 2, 5, 12, 15Read once at power-up to decide how to boot. Usable later, but a circuit that forces the wrong level at startup can stop the board from booting. Avoid while learning.
    Serial (UART0)1 (TX), 3 (RX)Wired to the USB bridge for uploads and Serial Monitor. Avoid.
    Flash-reserved6–11 (CLK, D0–D3, CMD)never useConnected to the module's flash; touching them crashes the program.
    Power3V3, 5VSupply pins, not signals. Never connect directly to GND.
    GroundGND ×30 V reference; all connected together.
    ENENReset line. Pulling it to GND holds the chip in reset.

    Recall (from chapter 2)

    Why must GPIO6–11 stay unconnected?

    No ESP32 GPIO is a 5 V pin. Think about what you saw under the shield.
    Yes. The program lives in flash and is fetched over those six wires while it runs. Anything you attach disturbs that traffic.
    That's GPIO34–39 — a different restriction.

    Chapter 4 · Making connections

    The breadboard

    Concrete question: a breadboard has hundreds of holes. Which holes are connected to each other, out of sight?

    Under the plastic are metal clips. Each clip joins a small group of holes into one connection point. Select a group of holes to reveal its clip.

    • Centre gap: separates the a–e side from the f–j side. Chips and buttons straddle it so their pins don't get shorted together.
    • Rails: by convention, red/+ for the supply (here 3.3 V) and blue/− for GND. The colours are a convention, not a connection — the board doesn't care which rail you use.
    • Wide boards: many ESP32 boards are so wide that, once plugged in, they leave only one free row of holes on one side (or none). Common fixes: two breadboards side by side, or connecting to the pins with female jumper wires.

    Predict, then check in the figure

    Which pair of holes is connected?

    Neighbouring columns are different clips. Same letter does not mean connected.
    The centre gap separates e from f. That gap is exactly what makes it safe to place a button or chip across it.
    Same column, same side of the gap → same clip.

    Apply

    You push both legs of a resistor into holes b10 and d10. What does the resistor do in the circuit?

    Right. Both ends are already connected by the clip, so no current needs to pass through the resistor. Parts must bridge different groups.
    Both legs share one clip, so the clip is a zero-resistance shortcut around the resistor.
    It's bypassed, but by itself that doesn't short a power supply. It just does nothing.

    Chapter 5 · Outputs

    Output: lighting an LED

    Concrete question: GPIO23 can switch itself to 3.3 V. Why not plug an LED straight between GPIO23 and GND?

    Recall (chapter 1)

    Before reading on: what does an LED need to light, besides the LED itself?

    A voltage source (here: a GPIO set HIGH) and a complete loop back to that source's 0 V side (GND). This chapter adds a third thing: something to limit the current.

    Meet the LED

    An LED is a diode: a one-way valve for current. Turned the wrong way, it simply blocks (at 3.3 V that's harmless). Turned the right way, it has a nearly fixed forward voltage (VF) — the voltage it “uses up” while conducting — about 2 V for red, about 3 V for blue or white.

    Here's the catch: once the voltage across an LED rises past VF, its current shoots up steeply. An LED alone doesn't limit its own current. That's the resistor's job.

    The resistor and Ohm's law

    The resistor goes in the same loop (“in series”) with the LED. The supply voltage is shared along the loop: whatever the LED doesn't use, the resistor gets. For a resistor, voltage and current are tied together by Ohm's law:

    V = I × R  ⇔  I = V / R  ⇔  R = V / I

    • V: the voltage across the resistor, in volts (V)
    • I: the current through it, in amperes (A) — careful: 5 mA = 0.005 A
    • R: its resistance, in ohms (Ω)

    For the LED circuit, the resistor's voltage is what remains after the LED: VR = Vsupply − VF. So:

    R = (Vsupply − VF) / Iwanted

    Worked example — red LED on GPIO23

    1. Supply: GPIO23 HIGH ≈ 3.3 V. Why: that's the pin's output level.
    2. LED: red, VF ≈ 2.0 V (typical; your LED's datasheet may say 1.8–2.2 V).
    3. Target current: 5 mA = 0.005 A. Why: plenty for an indicator; far below what the pin can supply.
    4. Resistor voltage: 3.3 − 2.0 = 1.3 V.
    5. R = 1.3 V / 0.005 A = 260 Ω.
    6. Pick a real value: resistors come in standard steps (…, 220, 270, 330, …). 220 Ω gives 1.3/220 ≈ 5.9 mA; 330 Ω gives ≈ 3.9 mA. Either is fine. Rounding up is the safer direction.

    Experiment: the current-limiting resistor

    Your turn (some steps done)

    3. R = ? (calculate, in Ω)
    Hint

    R = VR / I with V in volts and I in amperes.

    R = 1.1 V / 0.004 A = 275 Ω. Nearest standard values: 270 Ω (≈ 4.1 mA) or 330 Ω (≈ 3.3 mA). Both are fine.

    Misconception check

    Does the resistor have to sit between the GPIO and the LED, or could it go between the LED and GND?

    Current isn't used up or slowed along the way. In a single loop the same current flows through every part, so position doesn't matter.
    Yes. In a series loop the same current flows through every part. The resistor limits the loop's current wherever it sits. Putting it on the GPIO side is just a common habit.
    Both positions give the same current, so the pin is protected either way.

    Two ways to wire an output

    What would change?

    In wiring B, what happens when the code sets the GPIO HIGH — and why?

    The LED goes off. Both ends of the resistor–LED chain are now at 3.3 V, so there is no voltage across the chain, and without a voltage difference no current flows. LOW makes the pin end 0 V, so 3.3 V appears across the chain and it lights.

    Self-check: did you reason with the voltage difference across the LED rather than “HIGH means on”?

    pinMode(23, OUTPUT);      // make GPIO23 an output (a switch to 3.3 V or GND)
digitalWrite(23, HIGH);   // connect it to 3.3 V → current flows → LED on (wiring A)
digitalWrite(23, LOW);    // connect it to GND  → no voltage across LED → off
    How much current can a pin deliver?

    The ESP32 datasheet lists four output drive-strength settings of about 5, 10, 20 and 40 mA; the default is setting 2 (~20 mA). It also notes that the per-pin source current drops (from around 40 toward 29 mA at maximum strength) as more pins source current at once. Those are capability figures, not a target. For indicator LEDs, aim for a few mA. Larger loads (motors, relays, LED strips) need a transistor — that's module 4.

    Chapter 6 · Inputs

    Input: reading a button

    Concrete question: a button is just two pieces of metal that touch when pressed. It produces no voltage. So how does the ESP32 “see” a press?

    What an input pin actually does

    An input pin measures voltage (relative to GND) and turns it into a 0 or a 1. From the ESP32 datasheet, at 3.3 V:

    • at least 0.75 × 3.3 V ≈ 2.5 V → reads HIGH (1)
    • at most 0.25 × 3.3 V ≈ 0.8 V → reads LOW (0)
    • in between → not guaranteed; it might read either

    The input itself draws almost no current, so it barely affects what it measures. That's useful, but it has a dark side: a pin connected to nothing has no defined voltage. It picks up stray electric fields from your hand, nearby wires and mains wiring, so it reads at random. This is called a floating input.

    The fix is a pull resistor: a resistor that gently holds the pin at a known level when nothing else drives it. The button then overrides it when pressed.

    • Pull-up: resistor from pin to 3.3 V; button from pin to GND. Released → reads 1. Pressed → reads 0.
    • Pull-down: resistor from pin to GND; button from pin to 3.3 V. Released → 0. Pressed → 1.

    The ESP32 has these resistors built in (about 45 kΩ per the datasheet). You switch them on in code: pinMode(18, INPUT_PULLUP) or INPUT_PULLDOWN. Plain INPUT means “no pull resistor”.

    Experiment: the input lab

    What if we removed this part?

    The pull-up connects the pin to 3.3 V. Pressing the button connects the same pin to GND. Why isn't that a short circuit — and what changes if the pull-up resistor is replaced by a plain wire?

    With the button pressed, the loop is 3.3 V → 45 kΩ → pin → button → GND. The resistor limits the current to I = 3.3 V / 45 000 Ω ≈ 0.073 mA (73 µA) — tiny and harmless. Almost all 3.3 V sits across the resistor, so the pin is near 0 V and reads LOW.

    With a plain wire (≈ 0 Ω) instead, pressing connects 3.3 V straight to GND: a short circuit. Current is limited only by the regulator; the 3.3 V supply collapses, and the board may reset or the regulator may overheat.

    Self-check: did you (1) use Ohm's law on the resistor, (2) say where the voltage drops, (3) name the short?

    Interpret

    With INPUT_PULLUP and the button to GND, your Serial Monitor shows 0 while you press and 1 otherwise. Bug?

    Buttons have no polarity. This is the expected behaviour of a pull-up circuit.
    Correct. Released, the pull-up holds the pin HIGH; pressed, the button connects it to GND. Code handles this with if (digitalRead(18) == LOW).
    A broken pull-up would give a floating, random reading while released, not a clean 1.

    Recall (chapter 3)

    You move the button to GPIO34 and keep INPUT_PULLUP. What do you expect while the button is released?

    That would need a pull-up. GPIO34–39 have no internal pull resistors, so the setting has no effect.
    Right. Input-only pins lack internal pulls. Add an external resistor (e.g. 10 kΩ from GPIO34 to 3V3), or use a general GPIO.
    GPIO34 is input-only: reading is exactly what it can do. What it lacks is an output driver and internal pull resistors.

    The 4-leg button trap

    Button bounce

    When metal contacts close, they bounce for a few milliseconds, so one press can look like several quick presses to a fast program. For “LED on while held” this doesn't matter. For counting presses or toggling, you ignore changes for ~20–50 ms after the first one (“debouncing”). That's module 2.

    The code, line by line

    1const int LED_PIN = 23;      // output: drives the LED through 220 Ω
2const int BUTTON_PIN = 18;   // input: button to GND

3void setup() {
4  Serial.begin(115200);              // text channel over USB
5  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);
6  pinMode(BUTTON_PIN, INPUT_PULLUP); // switch on the internal ~45 kΩ pull-up
7}

8void loop() {
9  int reading = digitalRead(BUTTON_PIN);           // 1 = released, 0 = pressed
10  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, reading == LOW ? HIGH : LOW); // pressed → LED on
11  Serial.println(reading);                         // a probe you can watch
12  delay(20);
13}

    • 4, 11 Serial prints are your measuring instrument: they show what the chip believes the input is. That splits any problem into an “input half” and an “output half”.
    • 6 This line is part of the circuit. Delete it and the hardware is incomplete.
    • 10 Inverts the logic because the button is active-low.
    • 12 A short pause keeps Serial readable; it also hides most bounce for this use.

    Chapter 8 · Diagnosis

    Troubleshooting: from symptom to cause

    In the fault lab, different mistakes produced the same symptom. Real debugging means gathering a second clue that separates the suspects. Here's a method that works for nearly every beginner circuit.

    1. Power. Is the power LED on? With a multimeter on DC volts: 3V3 pin to GND ≈ 3.3 V? If not, nothing else matters yet.
    2. Link. Does a serial port appear, does upload finish, does Serial print anything?
    3. Input half. Does the printed reading change cleanly (1 → 0) when you press? If it's random → floating. If it's stuck → follow the button's loop.
    4. Output half. Force the LED on (digitalWrite(23, HIGH) in setup()). Measure GPIO23 to GND: ≈ 3.3 V means the pin works and the LED loop is at fault. ≈ 0 V means pin number or code.
    5. Change one thing at a time, then re-check. Compare every measurement with what you expected before measuring.
    Multimeter basics for this circuit

    Set to DC volts (V⎓, 20 V range if not auto-ranging). Black probe on a GND pin, red probe on the point you're checking. You're always measuring a voltage between two points (chapter 1). Don't use the current (A) setting across a supply: in that mode the meter is nearly a plain wire, so you'd create a short.

    Case files

    Case A · LED never lights

    Serial shows 1 → 0 when you press. The LED never lights. Your meter reads 3.3 V from GPIO23 to GND while you press. Most likely cause?

    The reading changes cleanly with the button, so the input half is healthy.
    Good suspect for “LED never lights”. But the button also returns through that rail; without it, pressing couldn't pull the pin to 0, and Serial would be stuck at 1. The clean 1 → 0 rules this out.
    Yes. Voltage arrives at the pin (output half OK in code), the input works, the shared GND works — what's left is the LED loop itself: polarity or a leg in the wrong breadboard column.
    Then GPIO23 wouldn't show 3.3 V when you press.
    Hint

    List the suspects from the fault lab that cause “LED never lights”, then cross out each one the Serial output or the meter reading rules out.

    Case B · Ghost presses

    Nobody touches the button, but Serial prints a jumble of 0s and 1s. Moving your hand near the wires changes the pattern.

    A broken button would stay open (reads 1 with a pull-up) or stay closed (reads 0) — not random.
    Sensitivity to your hand is the fingerprint of a floating input: the pin is picking up stray fields because nothing holds it at a defined level.
    That affects the LED loop's current, not the input reading.

    Case C · Won't start after a change

    For a new button you added an external 10 kΩ resistor from GPIO12 to 3V3. Now after a reset the program doesn't start (the boot messages show errors). Remove the resistor and it boots fine. Why?

    3.3 V / 10 kΩ = 0.33 mA — negligible.
    Correct. GPIO12 (MTDI) is sampled at reset; HIGH selects a 1.8 V flash supply. The WROOM-32E's flash needs 3.3 V, so the program can't be read. Use a non-strapping pin.
    The problem appears without pressing anything, and disappears with the resistor alone removed.
    Hint

    Look up GPIO12's category in chapter 3. When is that category's pin level read?

    Case D · Always on

    The LED is on from the moment the program starts and Serial prints only 0, pressed or not.

    A reversed LED never lights; and the stuck input reading points at the button side.
    Right. A constant 0 means the pin is always connected to GND, and the LED just obeys the code. Rotate the button or use diagonal legs.
    A missing pull-up gives random readings, not a solid 0.

    Case E · Barely visible

    Everything works, but the LED is so dim you can barely see it in a lit room. Serial is fine.

    Reversed means fully off, not dim.
    Yes: (3.3 − 2.0) V / 10 000 Ω ≈ 0.13 mA. Check the colour bands, or measure the resistor alone with the meter's Ω setting.
    GPIO23 is a general GPIO, and an input-only pin would give no light at all.

    Case F · No port

    The power LED is on. Your computer lists no new serial port, so you can't upload. What do you try first?

    BOOT affects what the ESP32 does after reset, but the port is created by the USB bridge before the ESP32 is involved.
    Correct order: cheapest and most common cause first. Power LED on + no port = data path problem (chapter 2).
    The lit power LED shows power is arriving. And replacing parts is a last resort, never a first test.

    Open question

    Symptom: LED never lights, Serial is stuck at 1. Two suspects from the fault lab fit. Name them, and describe one measurement or change that tells them apart.

    Suspects: (1) board GND not connected to the GND rail; (2) button wired to 3V3 instead of GND (and, separately, a broken LED loop).

    Separating test: measure from the board's GND pin to the GND rail with the meter on Ω (or continuity) with power off: no connection → suspect 1. Or follow the button's second leg: if it goes to the + rail → suspect 2. Also: in suspect 2, forcing the LED on in setup() lights it; in suspect 1, it doesn't.

    Self-check: did your test give different results for the two suspects? A test that comes out the same for both doesn't help.

    Final challenge: a door indicator

    Goal: a green LED lights while the door is closed; a red LED lights while it's open.

    Parts: DevKitC, reed switch (2 wires, no polarity), red LED (VF ≈ 2.0 V), green LED (VF ≈ 2.2 V), resistors of your choice.

    Constraint: a display already uses GPIO 4, 5 and 16–33. The only free GPIOs are 2, 12, 13, 15, 34, 35.

    Your tasks (write your answers — they're saved only in this browser):

    1. Choose a pin for the reed switch and a pin for each LED. Justify each choice and say which free pins you avoided and why.
    2. List every connection (like the table in chapter 7), including anything needed because of your pin choice.

    Hint 1 — the input

    Two of the free pins can only read. That's exactly what a switch needs — but recall what those pins lack (chapter 3/6).

    Hint 2 — the outputs

    The remaining pins include strapping pins. For each, ask: what level does it need at reset, and will an LED + resistor to GND push it the wrong way? (Chapter 3 pin details.)

    Hint 3 — the trap

    Is there a free pin where adding a pull-up resistor would stop the board from booting? (Case C.)

    Explained solution (try first!)

    1 · Pins

    • Reed switch → GPIO35 (or 34). Input-only pins are ideal for a switch and leave output-capable pins free. Because they have no internal pull-up, add an external 10 kΩ from GPIO35 to 3V3.
    • Green LED → GPIO13: the only free general GPIO.
    • Red LED → GPIO2: a strapping pin, but its default at reset is LOW (internal pull-down), and an LED + resistor to GND doesn't pull it high, so boot is unaffected. Note: on clones with an onboard LED on GPIO2, that LED will light too.
    • Avoided: GPIO34/35 for LEDs (no output driver). GPIO12 for the switch — its pull-up would make the chip select 1.8 V flash and fail to boot (case C). GPIO15 would also work for an LED to GND, but it's a strapping pin with a default pull-up that controls boot-log printing; GPIO2 is the cleaner choice. GPIO12 with an LED to GND would technically boot, but it's one careless pull-up away from a non-booting board.

    2 · Connections

    1. USB → board (power + Serial). Board GND → GND rail.
    2. GPIO13 → 220 Ω → green anode; green cathode → GND rail.
    3. GPIO2 → 220 Ω → red anode; red cathode → GND rail.
    4. GPIO35 → reed switch → GND rail.
    5. 10 kΩ from GPIO35 to 3V3 (external pull-up).

    3 · Resistors

    Red: (3.3 − 2.0) / 220 ≈ 5.9 mA. Green: (3.3 − 2.2) / 220 = 5.0 mA. 220–330 Ω both fine. Pull-up current when the door is closed: 3.3 / 10 000 = 0.33 mA, harmless.

    4 · Code

    const int DOOR = 35, GREEN = 13, RED = 2;
void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(DOOR, INPUT);          // external pull-up does the job; INPUT_PULLUP would do nothing here
  pinMode(GREEN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(RED, OUTPUT);
}
void loop() {
  int open = digitalRead(DOOR);  // 1 = open (magnet away), 0 = closed
  digitalWrite(GREEN, open ? LOW : HIGH);
  digitalWrite(RED,   open ? HIGH : LOW);
  Serial.println(open);
  delay(50);
}

    5 · Prediction

    Door closed → 0 (green on). Door open → 1 (red on).

    6 · Troubleshooting “stuck at 1”

    Causes: (a) the reed switch's second wire isn't on the GND rail (or the rail isn't connected to board GND — but then the LEDs would fail too); (b) the switch never closes — magnet too weak, wrongly oriented, or a damaged switch. Separate them: with power off, measure the switch alone on Ω/continuity while moving the magnet. It toggles → wiring; it doesn't → switch or magnet.

    Questions for a later review session

    Try these in a few days, from memory, before peeking.

    1. Sketch the LED loop from GPIO23 to GND and mark where the 3.3 V “goes”.

    GPIO23 (3.3 V) → resistor (≈1.3 V across it) → LED (≈2.0 V across it) → GND (0 V). The parts' voltages add up to the supply.

    2. Why does an unconnected input read randomly, and what are two ways to fix it?

    Nothing defines its voltage, and the input draws almost no current, so stray fields move it around. Fixes: an internal pull-up/down (INPUT_PULLUP/INPUT_PULLDOWN), or an external resistor (needed on GPIO34–39).

    3. Which free pin would you never give a pull-up resistor, and what happens if you do?

    GPIO12 (MTDI). HIGH at reset selects 1.8 V for the flash; boards with 3.3 V flash then fail to boot.

    4. The power LED is on but there's no serial port. What's proven working, and what do you test first?

    The power path works. Test the data path: cable known to carry data first, then the bridge chip's driver.

    5. You want an LED to be dimmer. Bigger or smaller resistor — and by roughly how much to halve the current?

    Bigger. Current = VR/R, so doubling R (e.g. 220 → 470 Ω) roughly halves the current.
