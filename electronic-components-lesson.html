    Module 1 · Chapter 0

    Electronic components: power, ground, inputs and outputs

    This lesson takes you from “I have no electronics background” to being able to read a small circuit, say what every part is for, predict what goes wrong when it is wired incorrectly, and find the fault with a multimeter.

    Where you will end up

    This is the circuit we will build up piece by piece. We call it the Busy Light: a little desk sign powered from USB, where pressing a button lights an LED. It is deliberately small, but it contains every idea this module teaches — a power source, ground, a chip with an input and an output, and the supporting parts that make it behave reliably.

    A concrete example of the goal. Someone builds this circuit and the LED is lit all the time; the button does nothing. By the end you will reason: “The chip lights the LED when its input reads LOW. So I measure the input with the button released. If it reads 0 V even though the pull-up resistor should hold it at 5 V, something is connecting the input to ground permanently — most likely the push button is rotated 90° on the breadboard, so its internally-connected legs short the input to ground.” And you will know how to check that.

    Learning outcomes

    When you finish, you should be able to:

    1. Explain voltage, current, resistance and ground with a mental model that predicts real behaviour — and say where the popular water analogy stops working.
    2. Identify the main categories of components (sources, resistors, capacitors, diodes/LEDs, switches, chips and modules) by symbol and by appearance, and state the job of each supporting part in a circuit.
    3. Calculate a current-limiting resistor for an LED and check that the resistor can handle the heat.
    4. Predict how a pin behaves as an input or an output — including floating inputs, pull-up/pull-down resistors and short circuits.
    5. Diagnose an unexpected behaviour by measuring voltages against ground and reasoning back to the cause.

    Chapter 1

    Just enough electricity

    A puzzle to start: a battery can sit in a drawer for a year and keep most of its energy. Put a single wire directly across its two ends, and the wire becomes hot within seconds. What changed?

    To answer this we need three quantities. Each one gets a precise meaning and a unit.

    QuantitySymbol · unitWhat it means
    VoltageV · volt (V)The electrical “push” between two points: how much energy each bit of charge gains or loses going from one point to the other. A battery maintains a voltage between its two ends. Voltage always involves two points — remember this, it becomes the key to ground and to measuring.
    CurrentI · ampere (A)How much charge flows past a point per second — a flow rate. Small circuits use milliamps: 1 mA = 0.001 A.
    ResistanceR · ohm (Ω)How strongly something opposes current. A wire has almost none; a resistor has a chosen amount; air has a huge amount.

    An analogy: water in pipes

    WaterElectricity
    ① The pump creates a pressure difference between its outlet and inletThe battery keeps a voltage between its + and − ends
    ② Litres per second through the pipeCurrent in amperes
    ③ A narrow section limits the flow (and the pressure drops across it)A resistor limits current (and voltage “drops” across it)
    Water only circulates if the pipe forms a closed loop back to the pumpCurrent only flows around a closed loop (circuit) back to the battery
    Where the analogy stops working.
    • Wires are never “empty”. The metal is already full of movable charge, so current starts everywhere in the loop at practically the same moment. Nothing has to “travel down the wire first”.
    • A cut wire does not “leak”. An open wire end simply stops the flow; charge doesn’t spill out.
    • Energy isn’t carried like a bucket of water. The charges themselves drift slowly; the energy is delivered by the electric field almost instantly.
    So: use water to picture difference, flow and restriction. For anything else, return to the real rules below.

    The rule that matters most: a closed loop

    Current needs a complete path from one battery terminal, through the components, back to the other terminal. Break the loop anywhere and the current stops everywhere. Also: in a single loop, the current is the same at every point. Components don’t “use up” current; they convert energy (into light, heat…) as the current passes through.

    Predict first

    The + side being connected isn’t enough. Current only flows if there is a complete path back to the battery. With the return cut, there is no loop — so no current anywhere, including through the LED.
    Exactly. Where the break is doesn’t matter. Try it in the experiment below and watch both current meters.
    Plausible from the water picture, but wires don’t hold a reservoir of moving current that keeps flowing. Without a closed loop, the current stops (practically instantly) everywhere.

    Back to the puzzle. In the drawer there is no loop between the battery’s ends — just air, with enormous resistance — so practically no current flows and the energy stays put. A wire across the ends is a loop with almost zero resistance, so a very large current flows, and the wire and battery turn the energy into heat. That is a short circuit. You will see it again in chapters 3 and 7.

    Recall

    An LED’s label says “2.0 V, 20 mA”. Which statement correctly reads it?

    Swapped. Volts measure a difference between two points (the push); amperes measure a flow rate.
    Right: voltage is across a part (between its two ends), current flows through it. That “across” versus “through” language will help you measure correctly later.
    An LED doesn’t store energy; current is a flow rate (charge per second), not stored energy.
    Which way does current flow?

    By convention, current is drawn flowing from + to − outside the battery (“conventional current”). In metal wires, the actual moving charges are electrons, which drift the opposite way. For everything in this lesson — and in almost all circuit diagrams — you use conventional current. The direction convention never changes a calculation as long as you use it consistently.

    Chapter 3

    Power and ground

    Every chip and module has at least two pins in common: a positive supply and ground. What exactly is “ground”, and why must it be connected so carefully?

    Voltage is always measured between two points

    From chapter 1: voltage is a difference between two points. To talk about “the voltage at a point”, we silently agree on a reference point and measure everything from there. That reference is called ground (GND), and we define it as 0 V.

    AltitudeVoltage
    Sea level is the agreed zero.Ground is the agreed 0 V.
    “The summit is at 2000 m” means 2000 m above sea level.“The pin is at 5 V” means 5 V above ground.
    Climbing from 500 m to 2000 m: a difference of 1500 m.Across a part from 5 V down to 2 V: 3 V across it.

    Where it breaks: sea level is a physical place, but circuit ground is just whichever wire we choose — usually the battery’s − terminal or the supply’s 0 V wire. And unlike altitude, ground is also part of the loop: current returns to the supply through ground.

    So ground has two jobs at once:

    1. Reference: the 0 V point every other voltage is measured from.
    2. Return path: the shared wire through which current flows back to the supply.

    Names you will see

    Positive supplyVCC, VDD, V+, VIN, 5V, 3V3 (= 3.3 V), red wire, red “+” rail
    GroundGND, VSS, 0V, V−, black or blue wire, blue “−” rail

    These colours are convention — helpful, not physics. A red wire connected to ground is still ground. Trust the labels and your measurements, not the colours.

    Predict first

    In the lab below, the black probe sits on ground (D) and the red probe on point B, between the resistor and the LED. Normally it reads about 2 V. Now the LED is pulled out, leaving a gap. What will the meter read at B?

    Tempting, but B is still connected to +5 V through the resistor. With no current flowing, there’s no voltage drop across the resistor (V = I × R with I = 0 gives 0 V), so B sits at the full 5 V.
    The 2 V was the LED’s own voltage while current flowed through it. With the LED gone, nothing holds B at 2 V.
    Yes. No current means no drop across the resistor, so B equals the supply voltage. This is a powerful troubleshooting rule: the full supply voltage appears across an open gap in a loop. Check it in the lab.

    Common ground

    Because a signal is just a voltage relative to ground, two devices can only understand each other’s signals if they share the same ground. If a sensor runs from its own battery and you connect only its OUT wire to a chip, the chip measures OUT against its ground — which has no defined relationship to the sensor’s. The reading is meaningless and often erratic.

    Apply

    A friend’s sensor works when both parts run from the same USB supply, but gives random readings once the sensor is moved to its own battery pack (only the signal wire was kept). What’s the most likely fix?

    The battery size isn’t the issue; the signal has no shared reference. A bigger battery changes nothing about that.
    Right. The signal is a voltage relative to ground; both sides must share the ground for it to mean the same thing. Note: you connect the grounds, not the two positive supplies.
    Connecting two different supplies’ positive pins can force current between them (they never have exactly the same voltage) — that can damage them. Sharing ground is what’s needed.
    Short circuit: power connected straight to ground. A wire (or anything with almost no resistance) from VCC to GND creates the loop from chapter 1’s puzzle. The only things limiting the current are the supply’s internal resistance and the wires, so the current is huge. Results: hot wires, a hot or damaged battery, a USB port that shuts off, a melted breadboard. A common accidental version: a jumper wire landing in the wrong rail, or the two rails of a breadboard bridged by a component leg. Before plugging power in, trace VCC and GND and make sure there is no direct path between them.

    Chapter 4

    Resistors and Ohm’s law

    Concrete question: connect a red LED straight to a 5 V supply and it flashes brightly, then dies. With a small resistor in the loop it glows happily for years. What does the resistor do?

    Ohm’s law

    For a resistor, voltage, current and resistance are tied together:

    V = I × R

    • V — the voltage across the resistor, in volts (V)
    • I — the current through it, in amperes (A)
    • R — its resistance, in ohms (Ω). 1 kΩ = 1 000 Ω, 1 MΩ = 1 000 000 Ω

    Rearranged: I = V / R (more voltage → more current; more resistance → less current) and R = V / I (what resistance gives the current I want?). Always convert milliamps to amps before calculating: 10 mA = 0.010 A.

    Why an LED needs a resistor

    An LED behaves very differently from a resistor: once it is conducting, its voltage stays at nearly the same value — its forward voltage, about 2.0 V for a red LED — almost regardless of current. Push a little more voltage and the current rises enormously. Nothing inside the LED limits the current. So we add a resistor in series, and let it take up the rest of the supply voltage. The resistor then sets the current by Ohm’s law.

    Worked example: the Busy Light’s LED resistor

    Supply 5 V, red LED with forward voltage ≈ 2.0 V. We want about 10 mA (bright enough for an indicator, safely below the typical 20 mA rating of a 5 mm LED).

    1. Voltage left for the resistor: 5.0 V − 2.0 V = 3.0 V.
      Why this step works: in a loop, the voltages across the parts add up to the supply voltage. The LED takes ≈ 2 V, so the resistor must take the remainder.
    2. Resistance for 10 mA: R = V / I = 3.0 V / 0.010 A = 300 Ω.
      Why: the resistor and LED are in the same loop, so the resistor’s current is the LED’s current.
    3. Pick a real value: resistors come in standard steps (…, 270, 330, 390, …). 330 Ω is the nearest common value above, giving I = 3.0 / 330 ≈ 9.1 mA. Rounding up the resistance errs on the safe side (slightly less current).
    4. Check the heat: a resistor turns electrical energy into heat at a rate P = V × I (power, in watts, W). Here P = 3.0 V × 0.0091 A ≈ 0.027 W = 27 mW. A common small resistor is rated 0.25 W (250 mW). A practical rule: stay below about half the rating. 27 mW is fine.

    On your own

    Independent problem

    A 3.3 V board must light a green LED (forward voltage ≈ 2.1 V) at about 5 mA. What resistor do you choose?

    HintSame four steps: voltage left for the resistor, then R = V / I with I in amps, then a real value.

    3.3 − 2.1 = 1.2 V across the resistor. 1.2 V / 0.005 A = 240 Ω. Standard choices 220 Ω (≈ 5.5 mA) or 270 Ω (≈ 4.4 mA) both work. Note how small the 1.2 V margin is: if this LED’s real forward voltage were 2.6 V, the current would drop a lot. Low supply voltages make LED current sensitive to the LED’s exact forward voltage.

    Why does this work?

    Your friend puts the resistor after the LED (between the LED and ground) instead of before it. Does it still protect the LED?

    This is the “current gets used up along the way” picture, which is wrong. In a single loop the current is the same everywhere (chapter 1), so the resistor limits it wherever it sits in the loop.
    Correct. Only the total loop matters. (Order matters for the voltages you would measure at the middle point, but not for the current.)

    Reading resistor values

    Through-hole resistors carry coloured bands: two digits, then a multiplier (number of zeros), then tolerance. Colours 0–9: black, brown, red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet, grey, white. Example: orange-orange-brown-gold = 3, 3, × 10 → 330 Ω, ±5 %. The LED lab above shows the bands for whatever value you choose. Bands are easy to misread (brown vs red vs orange in poor light) — when unsure, measure with a multimeter’s Ω setting before inserting the part.

    Two resistors in series: the voltage divider

    When two resistors R1 (top) and R2 (bottom) are in series between a supply and ground, the point between them sits at a fraction of the supply:

    Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)

    Example: 5 V with R1 = R2 = 10 kΩ gives 2.5 V in the middle. Why: the same current flows through both; equal resistances take equal shares of the voltage. This idea returns in two places: pull-up resistors (chapter 7) and light sensors (chapter 10). Its limit: the formula only holds if whatever you connect to the middle draws almost no current. A divider is good for creating a signal voltage, never for powering something.

    Apply

    You need to bring a 5 V signal down to about 3.3 V for a chip input. Which divider (R1 top, R2 bottom) does it?

    5 × 10 / (20 + 10) ≈ 1.7 V. The bottom resistor should be the larger share to keep most of the voltage.
    5 × 20 / (10 + 20) ≈ 3.33 V. This exact divider shows up again in the final challenge.
    That gives 2.5 V — half the supply. Might work as a HIGH on some chips, but it isn’t ≈ 3.3 V.

    Chapter 5

    Diodes and LEDs: one-way parts

    Concrete situation: you put an LED in backwards. Nothing smokes, nothing lights. Why does direction matter here, when it didn’t for a resistor?

    A diode lets current through in one direction only — like a check valve in a water pipe that opens for forward flow and shuts against backward flow. An LED (light-emitting diode) is a diode that emits light while current flows forward.

    Where the valve analogy stops: a diode needs a minimum forward voltage before it conducts noticeably — about 0.6–0.7 V for an ordinary silicon diode, about 1.8–3.3 V for LEDs depending on colour. And the “valve” has a limit in reverse: too much reverse voltage breaks it down. Small LEDs often tolerate only about 5 V in reverse.

    Why: what matters is the LED’s direction relative to the current’s direction. D1 conducts only when the battery is the right way round, so a reversed battery can’t push any current into the rest of the circuit — that’s reverse-polarity protection. Its cost is the ≈ 0.7 V it takes when conducting, which slightly lowers the LED current.

    Predict a measurement

    5 V supply → 330 Ω → LED (inserted backwards) → ground. Black probe on ground, red probe between the resistor and the LED. What do you read?

    2.0 V is what you’d read with a correctly inserted, lit LED. A reversed LED doesn’t conduct.
    That point is still connected to 5 V through the resistor, and no current flows, so the resistor has no voltage across it.
    Right — same rule as the open gap in chapter 3: no current means no drop across the resistor, so the full supply appears across the blocking LED. A reading of 5 V on the LED’s + side while it is dark is a strong clue the LED is reversed or open.
    Alternatives to a series protection diode. A Schottky diode does the same job with a smaller drop (≈ 0.2–0.4 V). A MOSFET-based circuit loses almost nothing (Module 2). Or use a connector that physically can’t be plugged in backwards — often the cheapest protection of all. USB connectors already guarantee polarity, which is why the Busy Light doesn’t include D1.
    Diodes across motors and relays (flyback diodes)

    Coils (motors, relays) resist sudden changes of current. When you switch one off, it briefly produces a large voltage spike in the opposite direction that can destroy the switching part. A diode placed across the coil, pointing “backwards” relative to the supply, gives that spike a harmless path. You’ll use this in Module 2 when driving motors with transistors.

    Chapter 6

    Capacitors: small, fast energy stores

    Concrete question: nearly every chip on every circuit board has a tiny capacitor marked “104” right next to its power pins. It doesn’t appear to “do” anything. Why is it there?

    What a capacitor is

    A capacitor is two conductive plates separated by a thin insulator. Charge can’t cross the insulator, but it can pile up on one plate and leave the other — and that stores energy. Its capacitance (symbol C, unit farad, F) says how much charge it stores per volt. Practical values are tiny: µF (microfarad, 10⁻⁶ F), nF (nanofarad, 10⁻⁹ F), pF (picofarad, 10⁻¹² F). The marking “104” means 10 × 10⁴ pF = 100 000 pF = 100 nF = 0.1 µF.

    Analogy: a small water tank next to the tapReal capacitor
    Tank sizeCapacitance C
    Water levelVoltage across the capacitor
    Filling through a narrow pipe takes timeCharging through a resistor takes time
    A full tank can supply a quick burst when the tap sputtersA charged capacitor supplies quick bursts of current

    Where it breaks: no charge actually flows through a capacitor’s insulator. Current flows into one plate and out of the other while the voltage changes. Once the voltage stops changing, the current stops: in a steady DC circuit, a charged capacitor behaves like a gap.

    Charging takes time: τ = R × C

    Charge a capacitor through a resistor and its voltage doesn’t jump; it rises quickly at first, then more and more slowly, because as the capacitor fills, less voltage is left across the resistor, so less current flows. The pace is set by the time constant:

    τ = R × C  (τ “tau” in seconds, R in ohms, C in farads)

    After one τ the capacitor reaches ≈ 63 % of the supply; after 5 τ it is ≈ 99 % — effectively full. Worked example: 10 kΩ and 100 µF → τ = 10 000 × 0.0001 = 1 s; practically full after about 5 s.

    Predict first

    In the lab below, you double the resistor. What happens when you charge the capacitor?

    The final voltage is set by the supply (5 V): charging stops when the capacitor matches it. The resistor only controls how fast it gets there.
    Right: τ = R × C doubles, so every point on the curve is reached twice as late. Verify it — the previous run stays visible as a dashed ghost curve.
    Only while current flows. As the capacitor fills, the current falls towards zero, and then the resistor has no voltage across it. The capacitor ends at the full 5 V.

    Choose

    Where should a 100 nF decoupling capacitor go?

    Yes. This is one place where physical layout matters even though the schematic looks the same. Schematics sometimes add a note like “place close to U1”.
    In series, it would block the steady DC supply (a charged capacitor is a gap) and the chip would get no power. Decoupling capacitors always go across the supply, from VCC to GND.
    Polarity and voltage rating. Electrolytic capacitors are polarized: the stripe marks the negative leg (also the shorter leg). Reversed, they can heat up, bulge and vent. Every capacitor also has a maximum voltage printed on it; choose one comfortably above the circuit’s voltage (a common habit: at least 1.5–2 × for electrolytics). Ceramic capacitors like the 100 nF are not polarized.
    Capacitors and bouncing buttons

    A mechanical button’s contacts bounce for a few milliseconds when pressed, so a fast chip may see several presses. A capacitor across the button (with the pull-up resistor, giving τ of a few ms) smooths the bounces into one clean change. Many projects instead “debounce” in software by ignoring changes for ~20 ms. Both are valid; software is free, hardware works without code.

    Chapter 7

    Inputs and outputs

    Chips and modules talk to the world through signal pins. An output pin produces a voltage; an input pin senses one. Almost every wiring mistake with chips is a misunderstanding of one of these two.

    In this module we treat the chip as a black box with four pins: VCC, GND, IN and OUT. A “digital” signal has two meaningful states: HIGH (near VCC) and LOW (near 0 V / GND).

    An output is a pair of switches

    Inside, a typical output pin is connected to VCC through one electronic switch and to GND through another. Exactly one is closed at a time: HIGH connects the pin to VCC, LOW connects it to GND. (The switches are transistors — Module 2.) This model explains three things at once:

    • An output can source current (HIGH: current flows out of the pin, through your part, to GND) or sink it (LOW: current flows from VCC, through your part, into the pin).
    • The switches are small. A pin can usually supply only a few mA to a few tens of mA — check the datasheet. Enough for an LED, not for a motor or a relay coil (those need a transistor, Module 2).
    • Wire an output directly to GND and set it HIGH, and the closed VCC switch is connected straight to ground: a short circuit through the pin. Same for two outputs wired together when they disagree.

    An input is a voltmeter

    An input pin measures the voltage on it (relative to GND!) and draws almost no current — think of a voltmeter with a very high resistance. It turns the voltage into HIGH or LOW using thresholds. A common pattern for chips is: above about 70 % of VCC reads HIGH, below about 30 % reads LOW. In between is undefined: the chip may read either. Exact limits are in each chip’s datasheet.

    The floating input

    Because an input draws almost no current, an input connected to nothing has no defined voltage. It “floats”: tiny stray charges and nearby electric fields — your hand, a neighbouring wire, mains wiring in the wall — push it around. It reads HIGH, then LOW, apparently at random. A button between the input and GND doesn’t fix this on its own: when the button is released, the input is again connected to nothing.

    The fix is a pull-up resistor (from the input to VCC) or a pull-down resistor (from the input to GND). It gently holds the input at a known level when nothing else drives it. When the button closes, the button’s direct connection wins, because the resistor only lets a small current through. This is the voltage divider from chapter 4 with a switch as the other half.

    Predict first

    Button wired from the input to GND, no resistor at all. What does the input read while the button is released?

    “Nothing connected” isn’t 0 V; it is undefined. 0 V would require a connection to ground.
    Some chips have an optional built-in pull-up that does this — but it must be switched on. Without one, nothing ties the input to VCC.
    Yes. Try it in the lab: choose “no resistor” and leave the button released.

    Diagnose

    A beginner connects an output pin directly to GND “to make sure it’s LOW”, then the program sets it HIGH. The chip gets warm and resets. Why?

    The opposite. With nothing limiting it, far too much current flows.
    Exactly. The pin’s tiny switch carries a huge current, heats up and can be destroyed; the supply may sag enough to reset the chip. Never tie an output directly to VCC or GND, and never tie two outputs together.
    Inputs are connected to GND all the time (via buttons, pull-downs). The problem is specific to outputs, which actively drive.

    What would change if…

    The Busy Light uses a 10 kΩ pull-up. What would change if you used 1 kΩ instead? And 1 MΩ?

    1 kΩ: still works. But while the button is pressed, 5 V / 1 kΩ = 5 mA flows from VCC to GND for nothing — ten times more than with 10 kΩ (0.5 mA). Matters for batteries.

    1 MΩ: uses only 5 µA when pressed, but the pull is so weak that stray electric fields and leakage can drag the input into the undefined zone — it starts behaving like a floating input, especially with long wires.

    So: the value is a trade-off between wasted current (too small) and noise immunity (too large). 4.7–47 kΩ is a common range; 10 kΩ is the usual default — a convention, not a law.

    Self-check:
    Logic levels between different chips. An output’s HIGH is close to its own VCC. A 5 V chip’s HIGH output is ≈ 5 V. Many modern chips run at 3.3 V, and many of those are damaged by more than about 3.6 V on an input (some are “5 V tolerant” — the datasheet says so explicitly). Connecting a 5 V output to a 3.3 V input is a classic mistake. Fixes: run both parts at 3.3 V if possible, or reduce the signal with a voltage divider (chapter 4), or use a level-shifter chip. The final challenge uses this.
    Built-in pull-ups

    Most microcontrollers have optional internal pull-up (and sometimes pull-down) resistors, typically tens of kΩ, enabled in software. They make the external R1 unnecessary — an equally valid alternative. The external resistor still has advantages: it works before your program starts, and its value is known. Some pins have no internal pull at all; the datasheet tells you which.

    How the breadboard is connected inside

    A breadboard hides metal strips under the holes. In the main area, each column of 5 holes (a–e, or f–j) is one connected strip. The centre gap separates the top and bottom halves, which is why chips straddle it — otherwise opposite pins would be shorted together. The long rails along the edges run the length of the board and carry +5 V and GND. Two gotchas: on many long breadboards the rails are split in the middle (a gap in the red/blue line marks it), and a 4-leg push button has its legs internally connected in pairs, so its rotation matters.

    Required or recommended?

    If you removed parts one at a time and tested on your desk, which ones could you remove while the Busy Light appears to work perfectly?

    Without R1 the input floats (random behaviour). Without R2 — left as a gap — the LED never lights; replaced by a wire, the LED is overdriven. Both are required.
    Right. On a short USB cable with a tiny load, the decoupling and bulk capacitors may make no visible difference. They prevent rare glitches and resets that show up later with longer wires, noisier supplies, or more load. That’s why they’re standard practice.
    Without the button there is no way to make the input LOW, so the LED never lights — the circuit no longer does its job.

    Chapter 9

    Troubleshooting: from symptom to cause

    When a circuit misbehaves, guessing and rewiring wastes time. Measuring is faster. The tool is a multimeter on its DC volts setting (marked V⎓ or DCV).

    The method

    1. Describe the symptom precisely. “Never lights”, “always on”, “flickers”, “dim” and “gets hot” point to different causes.
    2. Know what you expect. Write the known-good voltages next to the schematic (table below). A measurement is only useful compared with an expectation.
    3. Check power first — at the chip’s own pins. Black probe on GND, red on VCC at the chip, not at the supply. A good supply with a broken path to the chip is a common cause.
    4. Follow the signal: input → output → load. Find the first point where measurement and expectation disagree. The fault is between the last good point and the first bad one.
    5. Explain every reading. Your diagnosis must account for all measurements, not only one.
    Measure volts, not amps, while learning. Set the meter to DC volts and put the probes across things. A meter on its current (A) setting is nearly a plain wire; touching it across a supply is a short circuit, and often blows the meter’s fuse.

    Misconceptions behind many faults

    “The LED uses up the current, so less comes out.”
    MechanismIn a single loop the current is the same everywhere. Parts convert energy, not current. This is why the resistor works before or after the LED (chapter 4).
    “Ground is where electricity disappears.”
    MechanismGround is the return path to the supply and the 0 V reference. Break it and the loop is open; disconnect a module’s ground and its signals lose their meaning (chapter 3).
    “An unconnected input reads 0.”
    MechanismIt floats and reads randomly. Every input needs something that defines its level at all times (chapter 7).
    “A bigger supply pushes more current into everything.”
    MechanismCurrent is set by voltage and resistance. A 5 V USB charger rated 2 A only delivers what the circuit draws (I = V / R) — a few mA here. The rating is a maximum, not what it forces in.
    “It worked once, so the wiring is right.”
    MechanismFloating inputs and missing decoupling can work by luck and fail later. Check the design, not just a single successful run.
    “0 V on a point means it’s broken.”
    MechanismOnly compared with the expectation. OUT is supposed to be 0 V while the button is released. Measure in both states.

    Chapter 10

    Same principles, new circuit

    Before the final challenge, let’s recognise the same ideas in a different setting: a light sensor.

    A sensor is just a varying resistor in a divider

    A light-dependent resistor (LDR, photoresistor) has high resistance in the dark and low resistance in bright light. On its own it doesn’t produce a voltage — but put it in a voltage divider with a fixed resistor (chapter 4), and the middle point becomes a voltage that tracks the light. Feed that into an input (chapter 7) and the chip “sees” light. Many 3-pin sensor modules (VCC, GND, OUT) contain exactly this, sometimes followed by a small chip that cleans it into a firm HIGH/LOW.

    Transfer

    You swap the LDR and the 10 kΩ resistor. What changes?

    Right. Vout = Vin × Rbottom / (Rtop + Rbottom). Whichever part is at the bottom “gets” more voltage the larger it is. Same principle as choosing pull-up vs pull-down.

    An LDR isn’t polarized and has no power pin; it works in either position.

    Final challenge: the mailbox notifier

    Situation. You want a small box in your mailbox that lights an LED when the lid has been opened. You have:

    • A 3.3 V microcontroller board, powered by USB. Its pins include 5V (straight from USB), 3V3, several GND, and general-purpose pins (GPIO) that can each be programmed as input or output. GPIO pins use 3.3 V logic, are not 5 V tolerant, and have optional internal pull-ups.
    • A reed switch: a glass tube whose contacts are closed while a magnet is near. The magnet is on the lid, so: lid closed → switch closed; lid open → switch open.
    • A yellow LED (forward voltage ≈ 2.0 V). Aim for about 5 mA.
    • A vibration sensor module labelled VCC (3.3–5 V), GND, DO. Its DO output swings between 0 V and its own VCC.
    • Resistors and capacitors of common values.

    Your job: decide all connections, choose values, and plan how you’d debug it. The specific board’s pin numbers and current limits must come from its datasheet — the reasoning below doesn’t depend on them.

    Assumptions and simplifications

    • The chip U1 is a generic 5 V “black box”, not a specific part. Real chips differ in thresholds, output strength, internal pull-ups and tolerated voltages — read the datasheet before wiring.
    • LED forward voltages are treated as fixed typical values. In reality they vary by part and rise slightly with current, so calculated currents are approximate (roughly ±20 %).
    • Output pins are modelled as ideal switches to VCC/GND; real outputs sag a little under load.
    • The decoupling waveform and the LDR resistance range are illustrative shapes, not measurements.
    • Floating inputs are shown as random flicker; real floating pins can also appear stuck at one level for a while.
